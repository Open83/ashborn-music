# ASHBORN — MARATHON: Photography Brief (Filament)

2026-10-30 · @iCHARMS Tester

## Project summary

This brief asks for one real photograph: a cotton filament that changes from intact thread into a fragile ash structure that has kept its form. It is the master artwork for MARATHON, the debut single by the independent artist ASHBORN. A viewer should feel fragility and loss before reading any text.

- **The idea:** something may have been lost, but something remains. The artist's name suggests ash; the image shows what is left, not an event.
- **What it must not be:** a fire, a destruction scene or a sports image. There is no flame, smoke, ember or glow anywhere in the frame.
- **How it will be used:** Instagram Story and WhatsApp Status (1080 × 1920), Instagram square (1080 × 1080), Open Graph link preview (1200 × 630), and a website hero on desktop and mobile. Type (artist name, title) is added afterwards by the designer. Please do not add any. The photograph must stand on its own without text.
- **Sequence:** a mandatory test day comes first. The main shoot cannot proceed without ASHBORN's explicit approval of the test photographs. After the main shoot, ASHBORN separately reviews all final deliverables and approves the master portrait and landscape photographs. No artwork design begins before that second approval.
- **Creative direction:** approved. Photographic execution is not yet approved, and it will be judged on the real photograph.

### Emotional context — for visual guidance only

MARATHON explores an emotional, personal-journey territory involving pain, fragility, loss, memories and what remains after difficult experiences.

This is not a literal storyline or an instruction to depict specific events, people or personal experiences.

The FILAMENT — WHAT REMAINS concept should communicate through its physical material, composition, texture and atmosphere.

Avoid props or additional objects that introduce an invented narrative.

The purpose is to help the photographer understand the emotional atmosphere and avoid visual elements that contradict the approved concept.

**Status:** ready for photographer review and quotation, subject to the photographer's confirmation of technical feasibility. This is not final photography approval: the photographic execution is approved only after the test-day and final photographs are reviewed. Please flag anything you cannot meet before the test day. This documentation is locked for the current MARATHON release direction. Changes require a specific reason and explicit approval.

## Non-negotiables

These rules apply to the test day and the main shoot. If a rule cannot be met, stop and tell us before shooting.

1. **No active burning at the photography session.** Nothing is lit, smouldering or hot while the camera is running.
2. **Professionally prepared materials only.** All conversion of thread to char and ash is done beforehand by a qualified props or fire-safe specialist. The piece is fully extinguished and cooled before it enters the studio.
3. **No flame, smoke, ember, glow or orange light** in the frame. The scorched zone is a quiet brown, not a hot colour.
4. **No fake or procedural ash.** No textures, overlays, brushes, filters, 3D renders or AI generation stand in for the real object.
5. **No compositing of the subject.** Each frame is a single physical piece, photographed in place. Retouching is limited to dust, stack artefacts and sensor spots.
6. **No colour overlays.** The final colour comes from calibrated capture, not a tint laid over the image.
7. **The image is never dependent on a live process.** If the ash collapses, we shoot another prepared piece, not another burn.

You know the safety rules for your own studio and location better than we do. Follow them, and apply local fire-safety and ventilation requirements to the preparation space.

## Materials and production

The main risk is that ash holding its form is extremely fragile. Plan for collapse, and prepare spares and a still room.

## Camera and framing

You choose magnification, lens, working distance and aperture. The brief sets proportions and outcomes, not a macro ratio. Do not compromise the overall image to reach a particular magnification.

- **Priorities, in order:** natural proportions, readability on a phone, then fine detail.
- **Camera position:** top-down and parallel to the surface, with no wide-angle distortion. A longer macro lens helps keep the lens and lights clear of the subject.
- **Thread proportion rule:** the visible thread thickness, measured perpendicular to the thread's direction, should be **2.5–3.5% of the landscape frame width**, which is **3.75–5.25% of the portrait frame width**. Both frames are shot at the same scale, and the portrait frame is narrower.
- **Choose thread and scale together.** A 1 mm thread suits a frame about 30–40 mm wide. A 2 mm thread suits roughly 60–80 mm. Visible thickness matters more than the physical diameter.
- **Why the rule exists:** at 3% on a 1080-wide Story the thread is about 32–43 px. On a 390 px-wide phone web page it is about 12–15 px. Below about 2% it risks vanishing.
- **Focus:** shoot a single-frame reference first, at the aperture you judge best. Stack only if that frame shows soft areas in the thread, the twist or the transition at 100%. A flat-lay thread at a wider frame often fits within depth of field.
- **If you stack:** keep the unmerged source frames, and check for halos or ghosting on fine fibres. Those artefacts can look synthetic and must be retouched out.
- **Left edge:** a soft fall-off where the thread enters the frame is acceptable. The transition zone and the ash must be critically sharp at 100%.

Shoot each frame properly. Do not rely on cropping one frame into every format.

**Thread orientation.** Rotating the camera for Frame P turns the thread 90° in the image unless the piece is turned with it. Both compositions need the thread to read **horizontally** in the final image.

- **Option B (preferred)****:** a dedicated Frame P piece and a dedicated Frame L piece, **visually matched** and prepared in the same batch with the same method. Each is set in place on its own ground for its own composition.
- **Match them on:** thread type and diameter, twist, transition proportion, ash character, tip shape, ground, lighting, colour and treatment. The two frames should look like one photograph taken twice.
- **Option A (the same piece)**** is allowed only if** the photographer demonstrates, on the test day, that both compositions keep the horizontal thread orientation, for example by turning the piece and its ground together on a base without disturbing the ash.
- **Composition comes first.** Never compromise a composition to reuse a piece. If a piece collapses, shoot a matched spare and tell us which frame it was for.
- **Lighting follows the image.** Place the key light beyond the top edge of each frame as it appears in the image, so the light-to-thread geometry is the same in P and L.

## Composition blueprint

One slack thread crosses the upper-middle of the frame. About half of the frame is empty ground, and the lower third falls into shade, to give the designer natural tonal contrast to work with, not a reserved text box. Positions are percentages of the native frame.

**These percentages are photographic planning specifications only.** They set up the lighting and the frames. They do not decide the final poster typography. Once the real photograph exists, the designer sets the final composition by responding to the filament's path, the transition from thread to ash, the ash tip, the negative space, the natural light and shadow, and the overall emotional balance. The title must feel integrated into the artwork, not placed in an empty reserved box. The designer determines the actual text positions only after the master photograph is approved. Typography and its contrast testing are handled internally and do not affect your shoot.

## Lighting

The light should feel natural and cinematic, like soft window light raking across a table. Natural fall-off creates the shade zones, so do not fake them afterwards.

| Element | Specification |
| --- | --- |
| Key light | One large soft source, about 120 cm or larger, placed beyond the far (top) edge of the portrait frame, slightly to the left |
| Key angle | 8–15° above the surface plane, so it rakes across the thread and ground and shows relief in the twist, the fibre fuzz and the ash |
| Colour temperature | Daylight, 5000–5600 K. No gels, no coloured light |
| Negative fill | Black flag at the near (bottom) edge, to deepen the fall-off into the lower shade zone |
| Fill | A very low card on the left, holding the lit-to-shadow ratio at about 4–6:1 so the ash shape stays readable |
| Highlights | Soft. Avoid hard speculars. If the char shows a sheen, test cross-polarisation |
| Backlight and rim | None. No glow of any kind |
| Shadows | Soft contact shadow under the thread, falling toward the near edge. Keep the shadow detail open, not crushed |
| Texture | The ground's fine grain is visible at 100% but quiet. The twist, fibre fuzz and cracking in the char must be crisp, never smooth or plastic |

Test the light on the empty ground first (the clean plate). If the bottom 38% of the frame cannot reach about `#3A3631` naturally, tell us before the main shoot. That is a lighting problem to solve on set, not in retouching.

### Clean-plate lighting verification

Before the main shoot, test the final lighting on the empty ground for both Frame P and Frame L, and evaluate all three zones. A failing zone is fixed with light on set. Do not manipulate the photograph to meet a target, and do not report values from an adjusted file.

| Zone | Where | Pass condition | Report |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Upper shade | 0–30% from the top | No lighter than about `#4A463F`, with no hot spots | Darkest, brightest and average sampled values |
| Central lit zone | 32–56% from the top | Bright enough that bone thread and char both separate clearly from the ground, with no clipped highlights, and close to `#7F776B` | Darkest, brightest and average sampled values |
| Lower shade | From 62% down | No lighter than about `#3A3631`, ideally about `#2E2A26`, and evenly dark across the lower zone | Darkest, brightest and average sampled values |

- Sample from the calibrated RAW or the unretouched TIFF, with the colour checker shot in the same setup.
- Report the brightest and darkest areas of each zone, not only the average. A bright patch in the lower zone matters even when the average is dark, because text may sit there.
- Deliver the three-zone report with the test-day frames. The main shoot waits for our approval of it.

The overall palette is warm, desaturated and neutral: taupe, ash and bone. These values are **photographic targets, not overlays**. Calibrate with a colour checker in every setup, and the final palette is sampled from the real photograph.

## Reference descriptions

These describe a feeling and a standard. They are not images to copy. They are drawn from general knowledge of the photographers and fields named, and have not been visually reviewed for this brief.

- **Conservation-science macro of archival textile fibres:** neutral light, exact detail, an object treated with respect.
- **Irving Penn's still-life work:** tonal restraint, matte grounds, a small object given weight by the space around it.
- **Karl Blossfeldt's close-ups of plant forms:** the structure of fine forms, rendered as clear and quiet.
- **The feeling we want:** a small, fragile thing in a large, calm field, lit as if by a window. A viewer should sense that a touch would destroy it.

**Avoid**

- Stock "burnt paper" textures and anything that reads as a fire-safety or destruction image.
- Glossy surfaces, heavy bokeh, coloured light, or any warm glow.
- A straight, tense thread (it reads as a fuse), or a thread arranged into a shape.
- Props that suggest a story: letters, photographs, jewellery or names.
